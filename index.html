CANVAS · SAGE · Soil science

CANVAS in Portland: Fall leaves, soil moisture, and an SSSA Fellowship

Jasper A. Vrugt · November 2026

I booked the Hotel Grand Stark at the last minute for CANVAS, and it turned out to be much better than I expected. The walk to the Oregon Convention Center took eight to ten minutes, just long enough to enjoy the fresh morning air. Fall had definitely arrived in Portland. Leaves were everywhere.

Seeing all those leaves made me think of the Netherlands, where leaves on the tracks can disrupt train service. In Portland, I found myself wondering what they do to the hydrology. How much rain does a layer of leaves hold before water reaches the soil? And what happens when those same leaves collect over a storm drain? Eight minutes is plenty of time to get distracted.

The leaves also reminded me of work I did as an undergraduate at the University of Amsterdam. I wrote a computer model for how water and heat are stored and move through the A horizon, the upper soil layer enriched with organic matter. The model worked much as it would for any other soil horizon, with somewhat different hydraulic functions governing how the soil holds and transmits water. It was part of a larger effort to model water transport in a tree and use sap flow measurements to “train” the model.

My talk on soil moisture modeling with SAGE went well. It was a pleasure to share the work at CANVAS, especially after spending the walk thinking about what happens to rain before it even gets into the soil.

The highlight of the trip was being elected a 2026 Fellow of the Soil Science Society of America (SSSA), the Society’s highest recognition. I’m honored to receive it, and I took a photo of the award to bring home the proof.

My warm thanks to Jan Hopmans, professor emeritus at UC Davis, for nominating me, and to the colleagues who took the time to write letters in support. I’m grateful to all of you for making this possible.

Thanks also to CIROH for supporting the trip. And yes, the reimbursement request is in!

CIROH · Visiting Scholars Program · Research to operations

From catchment science to useful forecasts: reflections on my CIROH visit

Jasper A. Vrugt · October 30, 2026

I have just returned from my visit to the Cooperative Institute for Research to Operations in Hydrology (CIROH) as part of its Visiting Scholars Program. It was a wonderful opportunity to exchange ideas, strengthen collaborations, and reflect on how our research—including SAGE and SITEhydrology—might contribute to operational water forecasting.

CIROH connects water-science research with operational practice, bringing together hydrologic science, computer science, and social science to support NOAA in providing useful water information for flood, drought, and water-quality decisions and community resilience. Its Visiting Scholars Program supports focused research and education while fostering collaborations at The University of Alabama and across the CIROH consortium, strengthening the connection between research and operational forecasting. It was a great pleasure to discuss my work with the students and staff, hear their perspectives, and exchange ideas.

A special thank you to Pia van Benthem for her work in making the visit a success, and to Steve Burian, Executive Director of CIROH, for his leadership, support, and commitment to the Visiting Scholars Program. It was also a pleasure to see and work with Jonathan Frame, a former UCI student and co-author on many of our recent research efforts related to SAGE and SITE. He was an excellent host, and I greatly appreciated the time we had to work together and exchange ideas.

One highlight was visiting the operational floor inside the National Water Center and speaking with the people who actually make forecasts. During a future visit, I hope to spend more time learning from them: what goes into a good forecast, which uncertainties matter most, and where research can make a practical difference. I would like to better understand how we can help shorten the path from academic advances to operational implementation.

The flight home offered another perspective. Looking down across the United States, I was reminded of how much work remains to make our models more realistic. Mountains, forests, fields, cities, reservoirs, and river networks form an extraordinary patchwork. Even from an airplane window, the heterogeneity and intricate connections are striking. Representing these landscapes in a hydrological model—and making that model work across the contiguous United States—is a formidable challenge.

Thank you to everyone who made the visit so welcoming and productive. I look forward to continuing the conversations and collaborations.

Publication news · SAGE GUI · Environmental Modelling & Software

The SAGE GUI paper is now published

Jasper A. Vrugt · October 17, 2026

Our paper introducing the SAGE graphical user interface is now available online in Environmental Modelling & Software.

SAGE GUI: Fast and flexible differentiable hydrologic modeling with analytic gradients
Jasper A. Vrugt and Jonathan M. Frame
Environmental Modelling & Software, 2026, Article 107194
Read the paper · DOI: 10.1016/j.envsoft.2026.107194

SAGE GUI brings the complete modeling workflow into one application: preparing data, configuring hydrological models and neural networks, training, examining diagnostics, and exporting reproducible experiments. Analytically calculated sensitivities connect the hydrological models to gradient-based learning.

The paper describes 44 datasets across 38 regions, most corresponding to individual countries. Some collections span national borders, such as CAMELS_AT, which covers Austria and surrounding countries.

Development has continued since the version documented in the paper. The latest release adds CHAMELLION, covering catchments across multiple European countries, and CAMELS_SPAT, covering catchments in the United States and Canada. These additions bring the total to 46 datasets and expand the opportunities for cross-border hydrological modeling, as discussed in our first blog post.

Accessibility and transparency are central to the design. Researchers and students can configure experiments graphically, then export MATLAB scripts to inspect, modify and execute the workflow themselves. Windows and macOS applications run with the freely available MATLAB Runtime, without requiring a licensed MATLAB installation. Python source code and standalone applications for Windows and macOS are in the final stages of testing and will be released soon on GitHub.

We hope the paper provides a useful starting point for applying SAGE to test hydrological hypotheses and develop new theory about how catchments store, transmit and release water.

SITE update · Shared benchmarks · Second post

SITE Hydrology builds shared performance benchmarks

How optimization trials contribute better solutions across nine loss functions.

Jasper A. Vrugt · October 11, 2026

SITE Hydrology now uses global file updating to share improvements across runs and users. Results found on one computer can improve the reference solutions available to everyone through GitHub.

Every trial can improve several criteria

A run performs ntrials optimization trials using a selected loss function and algorithm. For each trial’s optimized solution, SITE computes all nine commonly used loss functions and compares them with the best results in the respective files for the same configuration. A better solution replaces the corresponding entry; otherwise, the existing result is retained.

This means a run that maximizes Kling–Gupta efficiency (KGE) can also improve Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE). For example, a trial might fall short of the stored KGE record but exceed the stored NSE record. SITE then updates the NSE solution while keeping the existing KGE solution. Each trial contributes information beyond the objective that guided its search.

Comparisons follow each criterion’s direction: higher efficiencies or lower losses. The files contain the best known results found so far, which may come from different trials, algorithms, and users.

Download locally and share improvements

For runs using the default SITE benchmark settings, the exchange follows three steps:

  1. Download: At the start of a run, SITE downloads the latest results files from GitHub.
  2. Compute and store: Trials run locally. Their optimized solutions are evaluated, and results are stored in local files.
  3. Upload: Improvements are uploaded to GitHub and replace the respective shared entries, making them available to subsequent runs.

The cartoon shows how an improvement found at work reaches a user in the watershed. Users at home participate in the same exchange; the phone illustrates checking shared results on the go.

Which results belong in the shared benchmarks

The GitHub results files cover all countries and regions represented by SITE’s supported datasets, the available time resolutions (15-minute, hourly, and daily), hydrologic models, and choices of meteorological products. Results are kept separately for each configuration so that comparisons use the same dataset, time resolution, model, and meteorological inputs.

Shared benchmarks use the default SITE settings for the training and evaluation periods, spin-up period, initial model states, and ordinary differential equation (ODE) variables. If a user chooses a different training or evaluation period, results are stored locally only, without updating GitHub. Restricting the shared collection to the default settings keeps comparisons consistent and avoids an unlimited number of results for custom configurations.

Our aim is to build a benchmark of each model’s performance for each dataset, time resolution, and meteorological product across nine commonly used loss functions. Contributions from different users can improve these benchmarks over time.

Each shared solution records the user, computer, and platform that created it. Its origin remains traceable as the shared collection improves, allowing SITE to use both the computational effort and discoveries of its users efficiently.

What’s new · Why SAGE · First post

From prediction to understanding: testing hydrologic hypotheses across countries

Jasper A. Vrugt · October 8, 2026

I developed SAGE to make it easier to test hydrologic hypotheses against large-sample datasets, in pursuit of new theory about how catchments store, transmit, and release water. Faster training is useful because it makes more scientific experiments possible. The ultimate aim is a better understanding of how catchments function.

The blog will combine capability extensions—new modules, models, datasets, and options—with project descriptions, practical tutorials, bug fixes, and release notes. I also want to share general observations and research ideas, discuss numerical and modeling choices, and feature applications and questions contributed by users.

My hope is that this blog becomes a place to exchange experiments and questions. If you use SAGE or SITE in your research, I would be interested to hear what you tested, what failed, and what you discovered about catchment behavior. This first post is longer because it introduces the purpose of SAGE and the scope of the blog. Future posts will be much shorter, each focused on one update, experiment, or idea, so they are easier to read and use.

What did the model learn?

We can train an LSTM with tens of thousands of adjustable parameters to obtain excellent predictions of river discharge. But what did it learn about the watershed? Which parts of its behavior reflect storage, connectivity, thresholds, or delayed responses—and which relationships will hold under conditions outside the training data?

Machine learning offers powerful tools for prediction and discovery. My concern is what happens if predictive accuracy becomes the sole measure of progress. Hydrology risks becoming a number-crunching discipline in which improving a score takes precedence over explaining the system that produced the observations.

A scientific discipline needs theory on which it can build. Theory allows us to state assumptions explicitly, derive testable consequences, design experiments that distinguish competing explanations, and revise our understanding when observations contradict it. It also gives us a basis for asking when a relationship should transfer to a new catchment, climate, or time scale.

SAGE as a framework for hypothesis testing

SAGE trains hydrologic models across a collection of basins. It learns a relationship between catchment attributes and model parameters, allowing information from the training basins to inform predictions in other basins. Analytic forward sensitivities provide the gradients used in training.

This makes it practical to express a hypothesis in a model’s equations, compare alternative formulations under aligned conditions, and examine whether their behavior transfers beyond the calibration sample. We might ask whether a different storage–discharge relationship explains recession behavior, whether a delayed response improves the representation of transport, or whether a proposed mechanism remains useful across contrasting catchments.

A good fit alone does not establish that a mechanism is correct. Different models can reproduce similar discharge records. Testing against independent basins and periods, examining model states and fluxes, and using additional observation types where available can provide stronger constraints. SAGE is intended to make that cycle of proposing, testing, rejecting, and refining hypotheses easier. For example, HYD-RESPONSES allows hypotheses to be tested against discharge, snow water equivalent, and soil moisture using the seven built-in models or user_model.

Extending those tests across countries

Most datasets available in SAGE are organized by country, with several countries offering more than one collection. Examples include CAMELS-US, MACH-US, and CAMELSH-US for the United States; CAMELS-KR and CAMELSH-KR for South Korea; and CAMELS-ES and BULL-ES for Spain.

Multi-country training was already possible in v1.0.3 with CAMELS-AT, based on LamaH-CE, which includes Austria and neighboring countries such as Czechia and Hungary. The new CHAMELLION integration brings together European catchments, while the combined CAMELS-SPAT integration covers the United States and Canada. These additions broaden the range of environments available for training and testing hydrologic hypotheses across countries.

These collections allow training and evaluation to span national boundaries within a dataset. Country boundaries are useful for organizing data, but the scientific questions concern differences in climate, soils, geology, topography, and hydrologic response. Does a proposed relationship explain behavior across those differences? Where does it fail, and what might that failure tell us about the processes missing from the model?

Development status: CHAMELLION and the combined US–Canada CAMELS-SPAT integration are additions being prepared for v1.0.4; they are not features of the published v1.0.3 application.

A basin-by-basin benchmark with SITE

SITE calibrates a separate parameter vector for each basin. It provides a complementary benchmark: how well can the same model perform when its parameters are estimated independently for each watershed?

Comparing SAGE and SITE helps us examine the benefits and limitations of learning across basins. The comparison is most informative when the model, observations, time periods, objective function, and numerical settings are aligned. Neither approach establishes a theory by itself; both support experiments that can challenge it.

Making experiments easier to repeat

I developed the graphical interfaces to make these experiments easier to configure, inspect, and repeat. The workflow brings together dataset installation, basin selection, model settings, training or calibration, and diagnostic plots. The computational source is also available for researchers who want to inspect or extend the methods.

Where to begin

Download the application for your operating system from the SAGEhydrology releases or SITEhydrology releases page. Start with a supported dataset and a small experiment before expanding to a larger basin collection. While you explore SAGE’s current capabilities, I am preparing v1.0.4 with AI-assisted modeling and developing two further modules: multi-objective training to explore Pareto trade-offs, and frequentist uncertainty analysis to quantify parameter uncertainty and assess model misspecification.
